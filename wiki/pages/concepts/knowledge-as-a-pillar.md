# Knowledge as a pillar of AI

The playbook's thesis fits in one line:

> Knowledge is a pillar of AI at scale, alongside data. It must be managed as infrastructure, separately from the way it is consumed.

The [[lew-platt|HP quote]] named, in the 1990s, a gap every large organisation recognises: a company knows far more than it manages to use. Expertise stays in one corner, know-how does not travel, people spend their time searching. The ambition it expressed has not changed: bring the right knowledge, in the right shape and quality, to the right place at the right time. What has changed is who needs it. A colleague can ask a colleague; an agent cannot. What a company knows but has not made accessible, an agent will never know, and an agent that is not grounded in that knowledge can only produce what is plausible, not what is reliable (see [[grounding]]).

The first stake is therefore context. An agent needs two things: data, what the company records, and knowledge, what it means and how to act on it (see [[data-information-knowledge]]). Every company that has tried to put AI to work has learned the lesson about data, "garbage in, garbage out", even if it is not quite that simple: data quality, ownership and governance are now standard items of any AI programme. Knowledge, here meaning what lets someone act well (the rule, the exception, the reason behind both, last year's trade-off and why), received less attention: the discipline exists, but it had no seat at the table (see [[underestimation-reasons]]).

AI also changes the volume. More content does not mean more knowledge: the more content there is, the more it needs to be traced, qualified and sorted.

Knowledge management itself is mature, up to an international standard ([[iso-30401]]). What it long lacked were tools people truly adopt. AI changes that: before it helps consume knowledge, it can help manage it.
