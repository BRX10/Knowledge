The Knowledge Mesh · Wiki demo · Version 2.5

The Knowledge Mesh Wiki

A demonstration: the playbook compiled into 89 interlinked pages, one idea per page, each with a short summary, its metadata, its sources and its backlinks. Browse it like a wiki, or plug an agent into it. The original document stays in Read.

How it is built

It follows the "LLM wiki" pattern described by Andrej Karpathy, which the playbook itself recommends: the raw source stays untouched, pages are compiled from it once and kept current, and a schema states the rules. The playbook is the source of every page: nothing is invented, in content or in sources. Every page is traced to the passage it comes from; figures are described in words and linked to their image.

Overview 1

  • The Knowledge Mesh PlaybookKnowledge is a pillar of AI at scale, alongside data. It must be managed as infrastructure, separately from the way it is consumed.

Concepts 39

  • AI agentAI agents are systems built on AI models that pursue a goal, use tools and act on someone's behalf. What they do depends on the context they are given.
  • Auditable knowledge constructionPrinciple 5, extending quality assured by the domain. Knowledge quality is read through traceability: who proposed each change, who validated it, from which sources, and how to revert it.
  • Authority, not probabilityWhat the owner commits to is narrower than truth: "this is what the company holds to be true, as of this date, and I answer for it." Dated, sourced, open to challenge. No owner, no authority.
  • Bases that coexistAs bases multiply, the answer is coexistence rather than fusion. Each base keeps its owner, charter and pace; bases share identifiers, metadata and a common way to be reached.
  • Codification vs personalisationHansen, Nohria and Tierney distinguished storing knowledge in documents from connecting the people who hold it. AI loosens that trade-off: the expert stays in charge, the machine does the clerical work.
  • Compiled knowledge (LLM wiki)The pages of a base are the compiled layer Andrej Karpathy described as an "LLM wiki": knowledge assembled once and kept up to date, rather than reassembled at every question.
  • Context engineeringSince 2025, practitioners have moved from prompt engineering to context engineering, the discipline of choosing what goes into a model's working memory. The Knowledge Mesh adds its organisational side.
  • Data MeshZhamak Dehghani's answer to the limits of centralised data platforms: four principles, presented as technology-agnostic, held together by shared standards and a common platform.
  • Data, information, knowledgeData records facts, information gives them meaning in a given context, and knowledge is what lets someone act on them. Knowledge carries judgement, and judgement always belongs to someone.
  • Distributed knowledgeKnowledge management research reached the Data Mesh diagnosis earlier: knowledge is distributed, created close to practice and kept alive by communities, which is why technology alone did not deliver.
  • Domain knowledge ownershipPrinciple 1, transposing domain-oriented ownership. Knowledge is embodied and partly tacit: the owner answers for it, not just stores it.
  • Dynamic contextContext assembled at the moment of use, from governed knowledge bases, within the rights of the person on whose behalf the agent acts. It is the consumption plane at its most demanding.
  • Eight failure modesThe same patterns recur when AI meets the real corpus. Each has a recognisable symptom, a specific cost for agents, and a principle of the playbook that answers it.
  • Federated knowledge governancePrinciple 4, transposing federated computational governance. The goal is not to merge bases but to make them composable, through shared identifiers, metadata and open access protocols.
  • Fundamentals before solutionsSolutions already address many challenges. The playbook starts one step earlier, with what must be true of knowledge for people and agents to rely on it; the answer shapes the choice of tools, and outlasts it.
  • GroundingGrounding is the shared basis on which joint action rests. Decisions and actions must be anchored in knowledge the company holds to be true, not in what a model finds plausible.
  • Interoperability and portabilityA knowledge base is built to last longer than the technologies that serve it. The test: if the tool that built a base disappeared tomorrow, could the knowledge be read, moved and served by another one?
  • Knowledge as a pillar of AITo understand a situation, an agent needs data, what the company records, and knowledge, what it means and how to act on it. Knowledge deserves the same status as data.
  • Knowledge as a self-describing productPrinciple 2, transposing data as a product. There are two kinds of consumers, people and agents, and no base without a charter.
  • Knowledge baseThe unit and asset of the mesh: small enough to have one owner and one purpose, complete enough to be useful on its own. It has seven parts.
  • Knowledge creation cycle (SECI)Knowledge is created as it moves between tacit and explicit forms: socialisation, externalisation, combination, internalisation. AI excels at combination and helps externalisation; socialisation remains a human affair.
  • Knowledge lifecycleKnowledge is born, lives and dies. Six stages mark that life, capture, consolidate, validate, serve, review and retire, each with someone responsible; retirement is the one most often forgotten.
  • Knowledge MeshA decentralised network of living knowledge bases, owned by business domains, each described by a charter and built through auditable contributions from people and agents, federated by common standards.
  • Knowledge OwnerThe person accountable for a base, and the central idea of the playbook: knowledge needs a face. Agents take on most of the stewardship; the owner keeps what cannot be delegated.
  • Organised fragmentationThe playbook's stance: accept fragmentation and organise it, rather than pursue the single base. Knowledge is fragmented because it is distributed, situated and alive.
  • Principle zeroManaging knowledge and consuming it are two different jobs, in two planes with different roles and rhythms. The separation concerns roles and rhythms, not teams.
  • Self-serve knowledge platformPrinciple 3, transposing the self-serve data platform. AI is not only hosted on the platform, it works inside it: it drafts, links and flags contradictions.
  • Start small, and realThe first steps aim at a first base that is useful quickly, owned by someone and measured from the start: one domain, one owner, a one-page charter, the legacy compiled, an evaluation set.
  • Tacit and explicit knowledgePart of what experts know will never be fully written down. A Knowledge Mesh aims to make explicit what can be, and to show clearly where the rest lives: with whom, in which community.
  • The challenges of a Knowledge MeshThe principles say what a Knowledge Mesh is; the challenges say what it must make possible. Fourteen questions fall into three families: managing knowledge, consuming it, and federating the bases.
  • The document as outputBuilding a base turns legacy documents into an asset and audits what the organisation knows. Tomorrow, documents are produced from the base; what is generated never flows back without going through the management plane.
  • The five principlesThe first four principles transpose those of the Data Mesh. The fifth extends the way the Data Mesh entrusts quality to the domains, rather than to central certification of golden datasets.
  • The mesh is a trajectoryNo company starts with a mesh: it starts with a common foundation, and the mesh appears as domains take ownership one after another. Centralise the means, decentralise the knowledge.
  • Three levels of delegationDelegating to agents is a gradual decision, taken domain by domain, as trust grows. Assistance, delegation and autopilot are each reached on evidence.
  • TraceabilityEvery element of an agent's context points back to a page, and every page to its sources and its owner, so that whatever an agent relied on can be inspected afterwards.
  • What agents changeThe fundamentals of knowledge management stay. Agents change three things around them, the reader, the scale and the economics, and the effort moves to validation.
  • What stays openThe playbook is meant to be extended by those who put it to work. Four questions remain open, about measuring value, the owner role, simplicity and a single entry point.
  • What the Knowledge Mesh valuesFive preferences sum up the playbook's answer. Each favours a way of working without dismissing the alternative: the mesh simply puts the first column first.
  • Why knowledge was underestimatedFour reasons, offered as a practitioner's reading rather than a measured finding: the model seemed to know, retrieval was treated as engineering, governance followed data, and knowledge management was out of the room.

Entities 10

  • Andrej KarpathyDescribed the "LLM wiki" pattern, the compiled layer behind the pages of a knowledge base, and the pattern this wiki follows.
  • AnthropicCited for two things: the notion of context engineering, which dynamic context extends, and the Model Context Protocol, as an example of open access standard.
  • EU AI ActRequires automatic logging, human oversight and log retention for high-risk AI systems; the playbook extends these as good practice for all agents.
  • Ikujiro NonakaTheorist of organisational knowledge creation who, with Hirotaka Takeuchi, described the SECI cycle; the playbook takes from him the insight that knowledge is created between people.
  • ISO 30401The international standard for knowledge management systems, cited to show that knowledge management is an established, standardised discipline.
  • Lew PlattChief executive of Hewlett-Packard in the 1990s, to whom the sentence "If only HP knew what HP knows" is attributed; it frames the gap and the ambition of the playbook.
  • Michael PolanyiIntroduced tacit knowledge with the observation that "we can know more than we can tell".
  • Michel de Montaigne"A well-made mind rather than a well-filled one": the playbook closes on Montaigne's ranking of discernment above accumulation.
  • Model Context ProtocolAn open protocol introduced in 2024, cited as an example of standard access: it lets any agent query any base in the same way. The protocol may change; the principle will remain.
  • Zhamak DehghaniAuthor of the Data Mesh (2019, 2020), whose architecture the Knowledge Mesh openly transposes.

Sources 22

  • Knowledge Mesh Playbook v2.5 (primary source)The primary source of this wiki: every page is compiled from it, and nothing is added. The other twenty-one sources are the references the playbook itself cites.
  • Dehghani (2019), monolithic data lake to data meshDescribes the failure modes of centralised data platforms: monolithic, organised around pipeline stages, and run by hyper-specialised teams cut off from the business domains.
  • Dehghani (2020), Data Mesh principlesFormalises the four principles of the Data Mesh: domain-oriented decentralised data ownership and architecture, data as a product, self-serve data infrastructure as a platform, and federated computational governance.
  • Tsoukas (1996), the firm as a distributed knowledge systemArgues that a firm’s knowledge is inherently distributed and cannot be surveyed or held as a whole by any single mind, management included.
  • Nonaka (1994); Nonaka & Takeuchi (1995)Theorises organisational knowledge creation as a continuous conversion between tacit and explicit knowledge, through socialisation, externalisation, combination and internalisation (SECI).
  • Wenger (1998), Communities of PracticePresents learning as social participation within communities of practice that share a domain, a practice and a repertoire.
  • McDermott (1999), why IT cannot deliver KMExplains why early knowledge management systems disappointed: information technology can store and move information, but knowledge is tied to people, their thinking and their communities.
  • Hansen, Nohria & Tierney (1999), strategies for managing knowledgeDistinguishes two knowledge management strategies: codification, which stores knowledge in documents for reuse, and personalisation, which connects people who hold it.
  • Davenport & Prusak (1998), Working KnowledgeOffers a practical definition of knowledge as framed experience, values, contextual information and expert insight, distinct from data and information.
  • Ackoff (1989); Frické (2009), the DIKW pyramid and its critiqueAckoff popularised the hierarchy from data to information, knowledge and wisdom.
  • Polanyi (1966), The Tacit DimensionIntroduces tacit knowledge with the observation that “we can know more than we can tell”.
  • DAMA-DMBOK (2017)Reference framework for the data management profession, covering governance, quality, metadata and architecture.
  • Karpathy (2026), LLM WikiDescribes a pattern in which a language model reads raw sources and integrates them into a persistent, interlinked Markdown wiki, noting contradictions and checking for stale claims, while a human curates sources and asks questions.
  • Anthropic (2025), context engineeringFrames context as a finite resource that must be curated for each step an agent takes, beyond the writing of a good prompt.
  • Anthropic (2024), Model Context ProtocolAnnounces an open standard that lets AI applications connect to data sources and tools through a common client-server protocol.
  • Montaigne (1580), Essais I, 26Essay on the education of children, in which Montaigne prefers a well-formed judgement to an accumulation of learning.
  • ISO 30401 (2018)International standard that sets requirements for establishing, maintaining and improving a knowledge management system in an organisation.
  • EU AI Act (2024), Arts. 12, 14 and 26Requires high-risk AI systems to log events automatically, to ensure traceability, and to be designed for human oversight; deployers must keep the logs for at least six months.
  • MIT NANDA (2025), The GenAI DivideReports that most organisations studied see no measurable return from their generative AI initiatives despite significant investment.
  • Platt, attributed (1990s)An attributed quotation, reported in several variants (“productive”, “profitable”), with no primary source located.
  • Furnas et al. (1987), The Vocabulary ProblemShows experimentally that two people choose the same word for the same object less than 20% of the time.
  • Clark & Brennan (1991), Grounding in CommunicationDefines grounding as the collective process by which participants in a conversation build common ground, the mutual knowledge and beliefs that joint action requires.
